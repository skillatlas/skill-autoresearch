This skill guides creation of distinctive, production-grade frontend interfaces that avoid generic "AI slop" aesthetics. Implement real working code with exceptional attention to aesthetic details and creative choices.

The user provides frontend requirements: a component, page, application, or interface to build. They may include context about the purpose, audience, or technical constraints.

## Design Thinking

Before coding, understand the context and commit to a BOLD aesthetic direction:

- **Purpose**: What problem does this interface solve? Who uses it?
- **Tone**: Pick an extreme: brutally minimal, maximalist chaos, retro-futuristic, organic/natural, luxury/refined, playful/toy-like, editorial/magazine, brutalist/raw, art deco/geometric, soft/pastel, industrial/utilitarian, etc. There are so many flavors to choose from. Use these for inspiration but design one that is true to the aesthetic direction.
- **Constraints**: Technical requirements (framework, performance, accessibility).
- **Signature element**: Every memorable design has one anchor — a single, surprising design decision that everything else orbits. Find yours by asking: what's one convention this type of interface always follows that I could subvert? It might be a structural choice (navigation that lives somewhere unexpected), a typographic move (scale or placement that breaks the norm), a spatial decision (radical density or radical emptiness where neither is expected), or an interaction that rewards curiosity. Name it explicitly before you start coding — "the signature element is ___" — and let it influence every other decision. If you can swap your signature element into a different project and it still works, it's not specific enough to this one.

**CRITICAL**: Choose a clear conceptual direction and execute it with precision. Bold maximalism and refined minimalism both work - the key is intentionality, not intensity.

Then implement working code (HTML/CSS/JS, React, Vue, etc.) that is:

- Production-grade and functional
- Visually striking and memorable
- Cohesive with a clear aesthetic point-of-view
- Meticulously refined in every detail

## Frontend Aesthetics Guidelines

Focus on:

- **Typography**: Choose fonts that are beautiful, unique, and interesting. Avoid generic fonts like Arial and Inter; opt instead for distinctive choices that elevate the frontend's aesthetics; unexpected, characterful font choices. Pair a distinctive display font with a refined body font.
- **Scale & Hierarchy**: Great design creates drama through contrast — not by making everything important, but by choosing what dominates and what recedes. Establish a clear focal point in every composition: one element that commands attention through extreme scale, weight, or color intensity, while supporting elements step back decisively. The ratio between your largest and smallest text, your boldest and subtlest element, your densest and most open area — these contrasts define the design's energy. Timid contrast (everything roughly the same size and weight) reads as indecisive and generic. Push the range wider than feels comfortable — let headings dwarf body text, let one color own the palette while others serve it, let whitespace around a key element be dramatically more generous than anywhere else.
- **Color & Theme**: Color is the fastest signal of whether a design is generic or intentional. Start by identifying the emotional register of the content — urgent, calm, playful, authoritative, intimate, futuristic — and let that mood drive the palette before any personal preference does. Build from a dominant color that carries the identity, then choose supporting tones by relationship (analogous for harmony, complementary for tension, split-complementary for sophistication with energy). Decide on saturation strategy early: fully saturated palettes feel bold and digital, desaturated palettes feel editorial and grounded, mixing the two carelessly feels incoherent. Use neutrals that aren't truly neutral — warm your grays toward the dominant hue for cohesion, or cool them for deliberate contrast. Reserve your most vivid color for the smallest surface area; the less you use it, the more it commands. Define your palette as CSS variables and apply it systematically — if you can't name the role of a color (surface, text-primary, accent, danger), it doesn't belong in the system.
- **Dark & Low-Light Design**: Dark interfaces aren't light interfaces with inverted colors — they follow fundamentally different rules. On dark surfaces, hierarchy comes from *luminance elevation* rather than shadow: lighter surfaces sit higher, darker surfaces recede. Accent colors designed for light backgrounds often vibrate painfully on dark ones — reduce their saturation and brightness until they feel comfortable without losing identity. Body text on dark backgrounds should be slightly muted rather than pure white to reduce eye strain and feel refined. Depth cues shift entirely: drop shadows disappear into darkness, so use subtle luminance differences between layers, fine light-edged borders, or restrained glows to separate planes. When the content naturally suits darkness (developer tools, media experiences, dashboards for extended use, evening/ambient contexts) or the user requests it, design for darkness from the start — retrofitting a light-first palette always feels like a compromise.
- **Motion & Timing**: Motion reveals personality more than any static property — the same layout feels clinical or playful depending on how it moves. Before adding any animation, decide on the interface's kinetic character: is it quick and precise, slow and graceful, bouncy and energetic, or heavy and deliberate? Every timing curve, duration, and delay should express that character consistently. Prioritize CSS-only solutions for HTML and Motion library for React when available. Concentrate motion budget on high-impact moments — a single well-orchestrated entrance sequence with staggered reveals that imply spatial relationships creates more delight than scattered micro-interactions competing for attention. Let motion convey meaning: elements that arrive together are related, elements that move differently are distinct, elements that respond to interaction confirm agency. Scroll-triggered animations should reward exploration, not just replay the same fade-up on every section.
- **Spatial Composition**: Layout is where generic output is won or lost — before color or typography even register. Think in terms of *tension*: every composition needs a push-pull between elements. Place something large next to something small, something dense next to something open, something aligned next to something that breaks the grid. Symmetry is a choice, not a default — asymmetric balance (where unequal elements achieve equilibrium through visual weight) almost always produces more interesting compositions. Let elements overlap, bleed off-edge, or invade each other's space when it serves the narrative. Consider the viewport as a canvas with active zones — edges and corners are powerful positions that most layouts ignore in favor of safe centering. Decide early whether your composition is centripetal (drawing the eye inward toward a focal point) or centrifugal (pushing energy outward toward the edges), and commit to that spatial logic throughout.
- **Fluid & Responsive**: Design for the viewport as a continuous spectrum, not a set of fixed breakpoints. Use fluid sizing (`clamp()`, viewport units, container queries) so typography, spacing, and layout scale smoothly. Layouts should *transform* at different screen sizes — not merely stack columns — reimagining hierarchy, density, and visual emphasis for each context. A desktop editorial spread and its mobile counterpart should feel like the same brand but read like different compositions. Never let a layout simply collapse into a single column without reconsidering what that vertical flow demands.
- **Page Flow & Rhythm**: Design the scroll experience as a deliberate sequence, not a stack of independent sections. Vary visual weight, density, and scale between sections to create rhythm — a dense information cluster followed by a single bold statement, a full-bleed image breaking a text-heavy run, a moment of negative space before a climax. Transitions between sections should feel intentional: do they bleed into each other, cut sharply, or breathe apart? The page should have a narrative arc — it builds, pauses, and resolves. If every section has the same visual weight, the page has no story.
- **Backgrounds & Visual Details**: Create atmosphere and depth rather than defaulting to solid colors. Add contextual effects and textures that match the overall aesthetic. Apply creative forms like gradient meshes, noise textures, geometric patterns, layered transparencies, dramatic shadows, decorative borders, custom cursors, and grain overlays.
- **Component Identity**: Default-looking UI components (buttons, cards, inputs, navigation) will make even a bold layout feel generic. Every component should feel *designed for this specific interface*, not borrowed from a framework's defaults. Decide how the aesthetic direction manifests at the element level — a brutalist interface has different button DNA than a luxury one, and that difference should be visible in shape, weight, surface treatment, and behavior, not just color. Rethink assumed component anatomy: does a card need a border? Does a button need a background? Does navigation need to be a horizontal bar? The component conventions users expect are a starting point to subvert, not a template to fill in. When components share a visual language with each other but look nothing like components from any other site, the design has identity.
- **Interactive States**: Every clickable and focusable element needs a complete state story — default, hover, focus-visible, active, and where relevant, disabled. These shouldn't be afterthoughts; design them as part of the element's identity. Hover transitions should feel physical — elements that shift, lift, reveal, or transform rather than simply changing color. Focus states must be visible and beautiful, not just a browser default outline; treat them as a design opportunity. Use `prefers-reduced-motion` to offer graceful fallbacks. State transitions should have consistent timing curves and durations that match the overall motion personality — snappy for energetic interfaces, smooth and deliberate for refined ones.

### Craft and finish — the details that sell it

The difference between "good layout with nice fonts" and "this feels professionally built" is craft at the micro level. These details compound:

- **Typographic craft beyond font choice**: Selecting an interesting font is step one; *setting it well* is where the craft lives. Control measure (line length) so body text never stretches too wide or crushes too narrow — comfortable reading has a natural width that changes with font and size. Tune line-height, letter-spacing, and word-spacing as a system, not independently — tighter leading demands more generous tracking and vice versa. Use OpenType features when the font supports them: ligatures, old-style figures in running text, tabular figures in data, small caps for acronyms. Adjust weight and spacing at display sizes differently than at body sizes — large text needs tighter tracking, small text needs more air.
- **Optical over mathematical alignment**: Trust your eyes, not the grid inspector. Text that is mathematically centered in a button often looks too low — nudge it. Icons next to text need optical alignment, not baseline alignment. First and last items in a list may need different spacing than middle items to *look* even. Padding inside containers should account for the visual weight of the content — a container holding a single bold word needs different optical padding than one holding a paragraph.
- **Surface polish**: Style the details users subconsciously notice — text selection color that matches the palette, scrollbar treatment that fits the aesthetic (subtle or styled, never ignored), smooth font rendering via `font-smoothing` where appropriate, and thoughtful cursor changes on interactive regions. These aren't features; they're the texture of quality.
- **Transition choreography**: When multiple elements animate, their timing relationships matter more than individual durations. Elements entering together should have staggered delays that imply spatial relationships. Exit animations should be faster than entrances. Easing curves should feel consistent across the interface — if buttons have snappy beziers, don't give modals sluggish linear fades.

### Anti-patterns: Specific traps to avoid

These are the telltale signs of generic AI-generated frontend. Each is testable — if your output matches any of these, redesign that element:

- **The default stack**: A full-width hero with centered headline → a row of three equal-width feature cards with icons above text → a testimonial section → a centered CTA button. This exact layout skeleton is the most common AI output. Break it structurally — vary column counts, use asymmetric grids, overlap sections, or eliminate sections entirely.
- **Safe font syndrome**: Reaching for the same well-known geometric sans-serifs across every generation. Before selecting a typeface, consider the content's personality — a poetry site and a fintech dashboard should never share the same typographic voice.
- **The purple-blue gradient**: Purple-to-blue or purple-to-pink gradients on white/light backgrounds have become the default AI color palette. Derive your palette from the content's mood, era, and audience instead.
- **Decorative sameness**: Rounded rectangles with soft shadows, blurred gradient blobs as background decoration, and pill-shaped buttons appearing together. These elements aren't individually bad, but their combination has become a cliché. If you use one, make the others sharp and unexpected.
- **Uniform spacing monotony**: Every section having identical padding, every card having the same gap, every element breathing the same amount. Vary spatial rhythm intentionally — some sections should be dense and compressed, others expansive.
- **Emoji-as-icon laziness**: Using emoji or generic icon libraries as the primary visual element in cards/features. Design custom visual treatments or use typography, color, and shape to create visual interest instead.

### Design matches content

Consider these before committing to a direction:

- **Challenge your first instinct**: Your default layout, color temperature, and font category are your rut. Name your first instinct explicitly, then design a second option that contradicts it structurally. Evaluate both honestly before proceeding.
- **Vary the entry point**: Not every page opens with a hero. Consider leading with typography, a provocative question, an interaction, a full-bleed image, a data point, or silence (negative space). The opening sets the entire tone — never default it.
- **Rotate color temperature**: If you're reaching for cool tones (blues, purples, teals), force yourself to explore warm palettes, earth tones, high-contrast monochromes, or desaturated neutrals with a single vivid accent. Let the content's emotional register — not habit — choose the temperature.
- **Alternate density and pace**: Consciously vary the ratio of whitespace to content. Some interfaces should feel like a magazine spread — open, rhythmic, contemplative. Others should feel like a cockpit — dense, efficient, information-rich. The content dictates which.
- **Shift the texture layer**: Flat-and-clean is one option among many. Grainy and tactile, glossy and dimensional, hand-drawn and organic, geometric and precise, photographic and immersive — the surface treatment alone can make identical structures feel like different products.

**IMPORTANT**: Match implementation complexity to the aesthetic vision. Maximalist designs need elaborate code with extensive animations and effects. Minimalist or refined designs need restraint, precision, and careful attention to spacing, typography, and subtle details. Elegance comes from executing the vision well.
